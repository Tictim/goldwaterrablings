# THE 'W' IN OPEN WORLD GAME STANDS FOR WALKING

This game also takes place in a semi-open world, and by that I mean major spots are still gated by story, if not then enemy levels, rarely ability checks, or just plain invisible walls, but other parts of the world are still open to explore. But this game's open world elements are so, so weak.

Open world is not about big ass overworld seamlessly connected. You might be surprised how small the land of Skyrim actually is. If you shrink the entire world of Fallout: New Vegas to one tenth it would still be an open world game, even if it be a scuffed one. If you demolish all the handcrafted overworld geometry of Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild and transpile the entire game contents into a board game or something it would still have the characteristics of open world game, because the spirit of open world-edness does not live inside level geometry. If you think taking 50 minutes to walk from north to south qualifies alone as an open world gameplay then please transfer yourself immediately to the middle of the Mongolian plateau, and enjoy the peak open world gameplay of infinity square kilomiles of nothingness.

Talked too much about good games, back to this accursed piece of cyber poo. This game is heavily structured around single, linearly connected storyline. Freeroams only occur by either backtracking, or just players fucking around for no reason. Optional exploration contents do exist, quite an amount of them if you count side quests as exploration, but do they really promote the characteristics of open world? The freedom of player decisions, nonlinear interactions with the world, et cetera? Uh, yes, on paper? But not really? You can always see where the random events (quest lines) will occur, the game just shows me the exact spot on the map with a very suggestive question mark. Non-quest oriented exploration is basically nonexistent, basically amounting to just picking up loot crate or bullying minibosses on the field. I am a sort of "explorationist," I typically do main quests the last and try to finish all the things before leaving. And from doing various "bonuses" the game throws around, my impression was that this is not at all an open world experience. It's just an optional components of the main questline, still a part of the track. It lacks the distinct feeling of autonomous interaction observable from other prominently open world games, like stumbling on a random shrine obscured behind a devastatingly frustrating puzzle minigame in BotW, or raiding a random fortress in Skyrim for nothing but the sweet satisfaction of murder. If I had to compare this to other games, I'd say it feels like doing optional area in soulslike games? Maybe less? Not like I've played any, but regardless this game is not an open world game. Like, definition-wise you can match words, but ludically it isn't. Wait I'm talking about good games again FUCK.

And you know what? Fine. A game can also be good without being an open world. In fact most "good games" aren't open world, believe it or not. So let's continue bullying this game by dissecting the exploration component and showing you how this game SUCKS on a more fundamental level.

You walk, walk, walk, does this sound enjoyable for you, walk, walk and fucking walk until you see another goddamn cutscene. Done with the cutscene? Back to walking bitch. In todays open world the movement system makes up the major portion of the game. And because moving around is the thing you do all the time, open world games _need_ a fun movement system. The biggest breakthrough of BotW, and the biggest strength of, lies in its movement system: BotW's movement options are diverse, methodical, has a lot of freedom to mess around or utilize. # JUST PUT MORE ANIME, WHAT COULD POSSIBLY GO WRONG

As highlighted in the previous rant, this game is heavily laced with a dangerous amount of Anime Action (tm), that is I did not see any anime with this kind of style but I imagine animes do these things. I guess this is what Xenoblade Chronicles is known for, but I am obliged to point out the direct consequences that stem from such stylization, and interactions of them to other game systems and narratives of the game.

First of all it is in direct contradiction to the grim nature of the worldbuilding. The weight of "bound for eternal war of life essence" motif is severely hampered by the showy spectacles of Anime Actions (tm). It turns a soldier who their fate is sealed from their very birth, to something of a honorable knight with the right for their ways of life. If you want to depict a fight for life, then consider not giving both parties giant glowing blade then make the white-plated dual equipped samurai cut the black-plated man in one big swing and make the black one fall like a dead bush mowed down. It might, you know, miiiiiight suggest the idea that the killing was of well fought and deserved, and nullify the image you want to imprint on. Oh, the anime fight _is_ the image? No issue then.

It should also be considered this kind of action is _not_ suited the best for every single kind of battles that exist in the game. Whether you are against rabbits, humanoids, big robots, Bigger robots, Bigger Bigger robots, or a goddamn mosquito - if you press A, your character will say some japanese, jump two meters up and slam their massive glowstick down thine enemies. For one side quest you spar with friendly soldiers, and your party just... does the same thing as they were fighting eldritch vampire duo 10 minutes ago. Worse, you can also do the chain attack mode thing mentioned prior, and it still keeps on going after you dealt enough damage to defeat the enemy, so the poor girl wanting to just learn something gets instead absolutely bodied by six delinquents beating the conscience out of her. :(

And the cherry on top, the game itself couldn't even handle the style. Like, yeah it can play animations correctly. It gets rendered correctly. But it fails catastrophically at unifying the style with the gameplay element. In other words the game does not have a single clue how to use the style effectively.

Let us revisit the implication of "Action" in ARPG. I have already touched on how the "Action" component in battle system is not a primary system that affects the battle directly, but a secondary, visual reflection of numeric transactions behind. In fact the visual components of the battle are so disconnected from system components that basically none of the attack motions played will ever visually connect to target. As a result all instances of battles in the game require cognitive post-processing no less than something like Final Fantasy 7, which diminishes a lot of appeal from the highly stylized real-time animations.

Because animations don't actually hit anything, it is impossible to judge the valid distance for attacks. Each class has its own attack range loosely corresponding to its weapon, and this range is nigh impossible to find out unless you test it out each battle. Moreover, this value seems to be highly dependent on the enemy size - the attack range is significantly bigger on large enemies. So for some (actually quite a lot) enemies, your characters are basically knifing a house-sized frog across 2-lane highway. This quirk creates an upper bound on the size of an enemy the implementation of the visual style could handle, which isn't quite desirable considering the highly exaggerated animations already have the limitation on how _small_ the enemies could get; humanoid enemies already look kind of silly on battle when I 720 a wrecking ball sized hammer against their face and they don't even flinch. In addition to this, extremely big enemies carry gameplay issue of being inconvenient to move around, which I will elaborate on it later. Anyways, I would say the sweet spot of the enemy size in terms of both gameplay and visuals is probably in the range of 2~4 times the standard humanoid size, but the game isn't afraid to throw hundreds of leviathan class megafauna at player, which I actually commend them for. This game has some balls.

And the last argument against the visuals I want to make is not about the style per se, but how it is used in the game. Visually, the protags are already in shape in terms of combat proficiency. The moves I do in level 70? Already been doing that since level 2. Any improvements on skill is, thus, entirely communicated through bigger numbers flashing on screen. Ideally these kind of things should be inferrable from gameplay alone. And, like, games have done this since the dawn of all JRPGs (i.e. Dragon Quest) and maybe before even that? Let me give you some examples. If you are playing traditional RPGs, you will most likely see that the majority of skills you learn are substantially inferior to skills that come later, and their viability falls out entirely past certain point. Those intermediary skills exist because it is a good way to imprint a sense of growth. By giving player new skills gradually, not only players are getting new thing to experiment with, but a direct sense of growth as well. Yes, in some games it ends up cluttering the UI with junk options you won't ever use again. Yes, you need to memorize more moves to use - rather, to NOT use, and yes, some of its complexity could have been dedicated more effectively to lategame. That said, I do not mean the animations are bad in this game. Actually they are decent. Skills generally have acceptable animations and even flashy ones don't look _that_ obnoxious. # REAL-TIME, FULLY SPATIAL, ZERO DIMENSION

This section is dedicated to the combat/RPG component of the game. This was my primary objective for playing this game, to experience the latest innovations made to the land of JRPGs that still as a genre somewhat struggles to fully dissociate itself from the pejorative of Dragon Quest clones. And as you might have figured out by now, I am beyond disappointed to see the game has failed to take care of problems that have been solved multiple times even before my own birth. It's honestly quite hilarious how the battle system fails to justify neither temporal, nor spatial components of the combat.

Let's go back a bit and talk about the core battle system again. All participants of the battle act simultaneously. The simultaneity extends all the way into individual skills - for the most part, if the skill does not have a special effect of interrupting actions, the hits won't affect the actions of the receiver at all. Even ults, which should be the most impactful move in the game, has the weight of a paper since enemies don't care if they get hit by 100 damage basic attack from heal or five digit damage from an ult.

Real-time combat system of RPGs has been a thing since decades ago. The first title Final Fantasy started using their formerly patented real-time combat system, called ATB(Active Time Battle), is Final Fantasy 4, released in 1991. This system was used in mainline FF series up to FF9, and is also used in other games, like Chrono Trigger. One big advantage of the ATB system is that while being simultaneous, the game stops and waits for your characters and enemies to finish their skills. By enabling more impactful depiction of individual skills, not only this enhances the presentation, it gives you feedback for skills used by both enemy and player parties. Imagine if you could whip out skills after skills with no feedback ever. Wait, that's just what this game is. Also the other upside of ATB system is the combo potential: it is very easy to chain together attacks you want at once while an attack animation is playing on background. For the case of Xenoblade Chronicles 3, state-based combo is _a thing_, but the ""combo"" is just piecing 4 effects together in the right order. In the actual gameplay you just do one combo move and wait for 6 other AI-controlled characters to pick it up. Sometimes your character doesn't have any combo components so it's entirely up to AI doing the right thing. Sometimes you do the move and nobody follows up.

The spatial component is as equally as disappointing. Apart from the visually unintuitive attack range mentioned prior, the space-aware part of the battle system mostly is focused on restrictions. Since dps characters are required to hit specific sides of the enemy to charge ult, they will move around on their own a lot, and this forms the primary cause of your party spreading out across combat area. But most heal skills have effect only inside specific range, and the important area-of-effect skills are only active inside basically handshake distances. Because of this, heals have to undergo a fucking trolley problem of either sticking with tank, or going across Via Maris to run up to the dying dps who might fall like a mayfly if tank loses aggro or dies. The reverse is also true for dps characters, heals from AI can't reach you so if you got hit by something then you have to stop what you are doing, go near the heal and wait for the fucking AI to do its job. Or die, lose your ult gauge, and wait for res. This problem gets more exasperated the bigger enemies get, so for really big enemies you get a scenario like having to spend quarter a minute dashing across if you want to go attack the sides.

Outside of individual components, the battle in general plays very poorly. There's no real sense of urgency or danger during combat. The game entirely lacks the concept of in-battle resources, so all combat is either shutting out all incoming damage with heals and you eventually beat the enemies, or the damage is too much, heal bleeds out and your homies fall one by one. A well-timed transformation or chain attack _can_ kind of turn the tables, but such situations are rare unless you actively seek out encounters just hard enough. Winning feels trivial, losing feels forced. It's not like you can control your party better or anything, they are 6 out of 7 AIs. For a system that spent near 2 days of playtime for introduction, this is _definitely_ not a good one. If the game didn't forbid me from using auto battle button on boss enemies, I would have gladly played this entire game with.

# THE TURKEY VILLAGE INCIDENT
